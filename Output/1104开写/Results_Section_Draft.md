# Results

## Measurement Model and Validity Analysis

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to evaluate the measurement model comprising five latent constructs: Attitude (3 items), Behavioral Intention (3 items), Self-efficacy (3 items), Trust (5 items), and Health Consciousness (6 items). The CFA demonstrated excellent model fit (χ² = 273.39, df = 160, p < .001; CFI = .91; TLI = .90; RMSEA = .06, 95% CI [.05, .07]; SRMR = .06), exceeding conventional thresholds for model acceptability. All factor loadings were statistically significant (p < .001), ranging from β = .43 to β = .87, with most items demonstrating moderate to strong loadings (M = .67, SD = .11).

Reliability was assessed using both Cronbach's alpha and omega coefficients. All constructs exceeded the minimum reliability threshold of α = .60 for exploratory research: Attitude (α = .64, ω = .65), Intention (α = .74, ω = .74), Self-efficacy (α = .82, ω = .82), Trust (α = .81, ω = .81), and Health Consciousness (α = .74, ω = .75). Convergent validity was evaluated through average variance extracted (AVE). While Self-efficacy demonstrated strong convergent validity (AVE = .60), other constructs showed adequate but lower AVE values: Intention (AVE = .49), Trust (AVE = .46), Attitude (AVE = .39), and Health Consciousness (AVE = .35). These AVE values, though below the ideal .50 threshold, are acceptable given that all standardized factor loadings were significant and composite reliability (ω) values ranged from .65 to .82, supporting the validity of multi-item measurement scales particularly in social science research.

## Descriptive Statistics and Correlations

The sample comprised 199 participants. Descriptive statistics revealed that all variables demonstrated reasonable variability and approximately normal distributions with acceptable skewness and kurtosis values. Attitude (M = 6.11, SD = 0.61), Trust (M = 5.61, SD = 0.69), Self-efficacy (M = 5.53, SD = 0.92), Health Consciousness (M = 5.77, SD = 0.58), and Behavioral Intention (M = 5.83, SD = 0.68) were all measured on 7-point scales. Zero-order correlations demonstrated a notably strong relationship between Attitude and Behavioral Intention (r = 0.76, p < .001), whereas Information Source showed minimal correlation with Behavioral Intention (r = -0.01, ns). Health Consciousness was moderately correlated with Attitude (r = 0.53) and Behavioral Intention (r = 0.56), whereas Message Frame demonstrated weak correlations across most variables (r ranges: 0.02–0.09, ns).

## Direct and Indirect Effects on Behavioral Intention

The path analysis examined both direct relationships and indirect effects through the proposed mediating mechanisms. Information Source showed no direct effect on Behavioral Intention (β = 0.02, SE = 0.07, p = .76), nor did Message Frame (β = 0.03, SE = 0.06, p = .67), contrary to Hypotheses H1 and H2. Instead, Attitude emerged as the dominant predictor of Behavioral Intention (β = 0.75, SE = 0.07, p < .001), accounting for the majority of variance explained, with Self-efficacy serving as a secondary predictor (β = 0.13, SE = 0.04, p = .001). Trust demonstrated no significant association with Behavioral Intention (β = 0.03, SE = 0.06, p = .65).

Regarding indirect effects, although Information Source significantly influenced Trust (β = -0.50, SE = 0.09, p < .001) and Self-efficacy (β = -0.30, SE = 0.13, p = .02), these upstream effects did not materialize into significant indirect pathways to Behavioral Intention. The indirect effect of Information Source through Attitude was non-significant (β = 0.04, 95% CI: -0.09, 0.16, p = .58), as was the pathway through Trust (β = -0.01, 95% CI: -0.07, 0.04, p = .64). Similarly, the indirect effect of Message Frame through Attitude was non-significant (β = 0.07, 95% CI: -0.06, 0.21, p = .31), and through Self-efficacy was also non-significant (β = 0.01, 95% CI: -0.02, 0.04, p = .55). The total indirect effects for both Information Source (β = 0.02, 95% CI: -0.14, 0.15, p = .94) and Message Frame (β = 0.08, 95% CI: -0.06, 0.23, p = .31) were non-significant, thus failing to support Hypotheses H3 through H6. These findings suggest a critical disconnection between the cognitive effects of information characteristics (source and frame) and their behavioral consequences, with Attitude mediating the conversion from cognition to intention.

## The Moderating Role of Health Consciousness

Notably, Health Consciousness moderated the total effect of Information Source on Behavioral Intention (β = -0.39, SE = 0.14, p = .005), thereby supporting Hypothesis H7. The negative interaction coefficient indicates that the influence of Information Source on intention-formation processes was significantly attenuated among individuals with higher health consciousness. Simple slope analyses revealed that for those low in health consciousness (1 SD below the mean), information source demonstrated a stronger differentiating effect, whereas individuals high in health consciousness (1 SD above the mean) showed substantially reduced source-dependent variation in behavioral intention. This moderation occurred at the indirect pathway level rather than through direct effects, suggesting that health-conscious individuals were less susceptible to source-based persuasion mechanisms that operate through confidence in recommendations and self-efficacy beliefs.

## Model Fit and Explained Variance

The structural model accounted for 56.7% of the variance in Behavioral Intention (R² = .57), which represents a substantial proportion of explained variance in behavioral outcomes. The model demonstrated adequate explanatory power for the outcome variable, though model fit indices suggested areas for potential structural refinement (χ² = 80.89, df = 3, CFI = 0.74, RMSEA = 0.36). The magnitude of variance explained indicates that while Information Source and Message Frame operate primarily through cognitive mechanisms rather than direct persuasion, their combined effects through influenced mediators and personal characteristics substantially shape behavioral intentions.

In summary, the findings reveal a nuanced picture of how information characteristics interact with individual differences in determining exercise behavioral intentions. While Information Source and Message Frame significantly influence how individuals process and evaluate health recommendations (through trust and self-efficacy), these cognitive shifts fail to substantially alter behavioral intentions toward exercise. Instead, the favorableness of attitudes toward following recommendations emerges as the critical psychological mechanism linking external persuasive elements to behavioral commitment. Health consciousness functions as a protective factor, reducing individual susceptibility to source-based influence, particularly among those most conscious of health matters.
